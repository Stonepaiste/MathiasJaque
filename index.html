Bachelor thesis · 6th semester · 2026

O.R.B.S.Together Through the Medieval Hellscape

Two custom spherical controllers that force players to work together — and a co-op game built to test them.

About the project

Our bachelor thesis asked whether tangible user interfaces can promote collaboration in video games. We designed the O.R.B.S. (Orchestrated Relationship Building Spheres) — two twistable, 3D-printed spheres — together with SpellBound, a co-op game where spells only fire when both players align the right symbols and press their spheres together. In a between-subjects study (O.R.B.S. vs. Xbox gamepads) using questionnaires, video observation and a Prisoner's Dilemma task, the O.R.B.S. group generally showed more collaboration — more explicit coordination, closeness and bonding — though the sample was too small to confirm it statistically.

What I did

  • Designed and assembled the hardware for both sphere controllers — everything inside the shells.
  • Electronics: M5Stack Stamp S3 (ESP32), a rotary encoder for 360° twist and press, a haptic motor and an LED ring that glows green or red when symbols line up.
  • Solved power and space problems — moved from a SparkFun Thing Plus to the smaller Stamp S3 and from 3.7 V to a boosted 5 V supply.
  • Wireless link: the spheres send input over ESP-NOW to a receiver ESP that feeds Unity over USB serial.
  • Ran the comparative playtests together with the group.

What I learned

  • Hardware lives or dies on power and space — the spheres only worked once I rethought them around a smaller board and a boosted 5 V supply.
  • Test the whole system, not just the new part. We usability-tested the controllers but not the game logic, so a button-mashing shortcut only surfaced in the final study.
  • Study design decides what you can claim: with 11 participants the trend was clear but not conclusive. Screening participants and filming hands and screen separately would have made the data stronger.
  • Custom hardware has to earn its place — a small effect raises the fair question of whether a custom game alone could do the job.

Game design · 6th semester · 2026

Deepseek — Team Deep

An underwater horror-survival extraction game where sound does the heavy lifting.

About the project

A single-player adventure game: you pilot a diving vessel across a dark ocean floor, find and service drill stations and solve puzzles while the threat level rises. We took it from paper prototype to a playable Unity build, with playtests shaping the balance, accessibility and narrative.

What I did

  • All sound implementation in Wwise, and most of the sound design.
  • Sound vision: every sound should feel like it's heard through real water, reinforcing the visuals.
  • Built an underwater microphone from a piezo element and recorded sound effects through water in a tank.

What I learned

  • How to plan and carry a shared vision for a game all the way through production.
  • Prototype on paper before going digital — it is far cheaper to find problems there.
  • Game design is iterative at heart: most pain can be avoided by getting players involved as early as possible.

AI programming · 6th semester · 2026

Duck and Cover — The Boids Are Coming

Flocks of angry birds, driven by behaviour graphs, hunting the player from above.

About the project

A Unity vertical slice: you run around collecting objects while six flocks of birds roam overhead. If a flock spots you, its leader dives in with a deadly firebolt — unless you reach cover first.

What I did

  • Individual project — design and implementation.
  • Flocking with boids (separation, alignment, cohesion), each flock following a "Masterbird" leader, plus obstacle avoidance against terrain and cover.
  • Unity Behavior Graph driving the leader through idle, roam, seek and dive.
  • Line-of-sight perception with raycasts, so taking cover actually protects you.

What I learned

  • How believable flocking emerges from three simple rules — and how much of the result comes from tuning the parameters.
  • Splitting "what to do" (behaviour graph) from "how to move" (motor script), and writing custom behaviour nodes that bridge the two.
  • Using an AI assistant to get unstuck on vector maths and syntax, while understanding the code well enough to keep iterating on it myself.

UX · Individual exam · 2026

Barriers to Smart Home Adoption

Why non-tech-savvy people still don't have smart homes — and whether AI could change that.

About the project

A qualitative, exploratory study with four Danish participants aged 33–67. Interviews and card sorting revealed five barriers: privacy scepticism (ranked most important by everyone), complexity, distrust in big tech, relevance in context, and Home Assistant as an alternative. Imagining AI-guided setup raised perceived ease of use (+6.8%) but lowered perceived usefulness (−2.4%) — AI alone can't fix a trust problem.

What I did

  • Individual project — research design, interviews, card sorting and analysis.
  • Semi-structured interviews and two rounds of card sorting.
  • Thematic analysis framed by the Technology Acceptance Model and Diffusion of Innovation.
  • Concluded that local, offline-capable platforms with transparent data practices matter more than smarter technology.

What I learned

  • Running a qualitative study end to end on my own — and how easily a lone researcher's expectations can steer follow-up questions.
  • Small convenience samples give insight, not general truths.
  • Method details matter: re-sorting the same cards after the AI discussion may have nudged answers — next time, clear the board and start over.
  • Trust beats features — people's barrier was privacy, not missing technology.

5th semester · 2025

Tacet VR

A virtual-reality experience of Ahmed El Kholy's short film Tacet.

About the project

We turned a short film into a VR experience — instead of watching the story on a screen, you step inside its dreamlike, fog-filled scenes. It was an extension of the film rather than a direct translation. We wanted to research how using VR could help raising immersion. 

Built in Unity together with the film's director, using environmental storytelling, spatial audio and cinematic lighting. An A/B study (VR vs. watching the film) with interviews and the Igroup Presence Questionnaire found no significant gain in how accurately people read the director's intent — but a clear shift in engagement: VR participants told the story from an embodied, first-person view, while viewers stayed observers.

What I did

  • Sound design and sound geometrics implementation via FMOD and meta XR sound kit to achieve realistic spatial sound utilising general HRTF's.

What I learned

  • Sound perception and cognition, how sound are physically perceived and modelled. 
  • Sound implementation using FMOD and Unity. 
  • Foley recording a anachoic chamber to be able to correctly use HRTF's (Head Related Tranfer Functions)

4th semester · 2025

IPASIF — Interactive Sound Installation Framework

A modular, wireless toolkit that helps artists build interactive sound installations.

About the project

A framework that links sensors on ESP32 boards to PureData for sound and TouchDesigner for visuals — ESP-NOW between the boards, OSC to the computer. Three use cases ("Spaceship", "Ballin'" and "What's in the Box") show what it can do; artists evaluated it in interviews, and stress tests measured about 44 ms average latency with satisfactory range.

What I did

  • Sensor design and assembly + code.

What I learned

  • People judge the demo, not the tool — the artists responded to our use cases rather than the framework, so testers need hands-on time with the actual toolkit.
  • There's a big gap between "it works" and "artists can use it": enclosures, ready-made sensors and a GUI matter as much as the core system.
  • Wireless latency is a real design constraint when sound has to react to people live. We could have stripping the first 5 layers of the OSI model and stayed within the ESP-NOW and serial protocols, which could have lowered the latency to around 1 ms. We used this method in future projects.

3rd semester · 2024

My Shadow World

A game about loneliness, told through its environment and story.

About the project

Loneliness is rising in Denmark, especially among 18–34-year-olds — who are also the group that plays the most games. We designed an exploration game where environment and storytelling carry the feeling of loneliness, built it through iterative prototypes and tested the final version with 27 players (think-aloud, interviews, SUS). Players recognised the feeling — and showed us how personal loneliness is.

What I did

  • ✏️ Add what you did on this project
  • Six-person group project.

What I learned

  • Visual computing can tell a story: shaders gave the shadow monsters no physical form, mirroring emptiness, and motion capture made their animation efficient.
  • Playing alone in first person deepened the experience — players felt isolated and explored more.
  • Combining qualitative and quantitative methods (think-aloud, interviews, SUS) gives a fuller picture — and loneliness is experienced very differently from person to person.

2nd semester · 2024

Night Knight — Safety App

Helping women aged 18–30 feel safer on the way home at night.

About the project

A personal-safety app designed in Figma and built in .NET MAUI, with live maps, trusted contacts and a motion-sensor alarm. Evaluated with 18 users from the target group (think-aloud, interviews, SUS) — scoring 85.9 on the System Usability Scale (grade A+).

What I did

  • ✏️ Add what you did on this project
  • Six-person group project.

What I learned

  • Pick the tool that fits the job: we started in Unity, but a game engine fought us on maps and app UI, so we switched to .NET MAUI.
  • Scope to what's feasible: detecting attacks with machine learning was out of reach, so we built a simpler accelerometer-based alarm that actually worked.
  • Easy to use isn't the same as useful — the app scored A+ on usability, yet most testers were neutral about using it often.
